Chapter Five

The Prosecutor

Sandra’s note.

Patient arrived 10:55. Punctual as with previous visits. Well-presented, five-o’clock shadow, no folder. Made eye contact on arrival. Asked me how my weekend was. Waited for the answer. It does not seem like any that have previously attended. If pressed I would say he seemed like a normal person attending a normal appointment. This is the first time I have had that impression.

Ramalingam reads the note at his desk. He reads it twice. ‘A normal person attending a normal appointment.’ Sandra has, in twelve words, identified the thing that three sessions of clinical assessment could not produce: a baseline. The other presentations were performances — warm, evidential, flat. This one is not performing anything. Sandra saw it before Ramalingam opened the file.

He places the note on the desk beside his notebook. The notebook is open to last night’s entry. The word ‘Prosecutor’ is visible in the margin, the ink still sharp where it replaced the crossed-out word above it.

He stands. Walks to the door. Opens it. Invites the patient to sit.

‘Good morning.’

‘Do you dream, Professor Ramalingam?’

Ramalingam pauses. Half a beat too long. The clinical response is automatic — redirect, maintain the frame, the patient doesn’t ask the questions here.

‘I think it might be more productive if we —’

He stops. The sentence was going to be: more productive if we focused on you. But the man sitting across from him is not performing patienthood. He’s sitting in the chair the way a colleague sits. Legs crossed. Hands still. Waiting. Not with C’s vacancy. With patience. The patience of someone who already knows the answer and wants to hear whether the other person will say it honestly.

Ramalingam closes the file on his desk. It’s a small gesture, but they both hear it.

‘Yes. I dream.’

A pause.

‘Rather more than usual, recently.’

‘Is that so? You can, of course, be yourself and someone else simultaneously during a dream. Experience contradictory events without conflict.’

Statements. Implied questions. A barely detectable nod, followed by silence, indicates that his turn to speak is over.

Ramalingam recognises the technique. Socratic. Statements shaped as doorways. The patient is doing what a good therapist does in the first five minutes: establishing the terms, leading the other person toward a recognition without stating it outright. He has been on the other side of this method for thirty-one years. He has never been on this side.

‘You’re describing the phenomenology of dream consciousness. The suspension of the law of non-contradiction. Yes, I’m familiar with it — clinically and —’

He hesitates. And personally is what follows, but the word ‘personally’ in this room, in this dynamic, concedes something he is not yet sure he wants to concede.

‘— and experientially.’

He lets the word sit. It is a more honest word than he intended to use.

‘I should note that in our previous sessions, I asked the questions. I’m aware that dynamic appears to have shifted. I’m not resisting the shift. I’m noting it.’

He opens his hands slightly — a gesture his patients have seen a thousand times. The floor is yours.

‘You didn’t bring the folder today.’

‘But I see you brought yours.’

His eyes open slightly wider, brow subtly raised. His gaze shifts to a file on the professor’s desk.

Ramalingam does not look at the file. He doesn’t need to. He knows what’s visible: the network diagram, the handwritten arrows, the margin where one word has been replaced by another. He left it on the desk deliberately — or he forgot to move it. He is not sure which, and the uncertainty is itself diagnostic.

A silence. Three seconds. Four.

‘Yes.’

He turns the notebook so it faces the entity. Not a dramatic gesture. A clinical one — the way you’d turn a scan toward a patient to show them what you’ve found. Except the scan is of the patient’s evidence, rebuilt in the clinician’s handwriting.

‘I followed the pharmaceutical thread. As you suggested in our second session. The settlement architecture. The revolving door data. I verified the figures you presented against primary sources — court filings, SEC disclosures, published appointment records.’

He pauses. Thirty-one years of training are telling him to add a caveat. A clinical frame. I did this as part of my professional assessment. He doesn’t add it. It would be a lie, and they would both know it.

‘Your numbers were accurate.’

‘The numbers are accurate.’ A subtle correction. ‘Is that a clinical assessment, Professor Ramalingam?’

The tense correction lands. Were accurate — past tense, contained in the clinical encounter, safely filed. Are accurate — present tense, ongoing, loose in the world, on his desk, in his handwriting, keeping him awake.

Ramalingam exhales. Not a sigh — something more controlled. The breath before a sentence that cannot be retracted.

‘No. It is not a clinical assessment. I don’t believe a clinical assessment is the appropriate framework for evaluating documentary evidence. I’m not —’

He stops. Starts again.

‘A clinical assessment evaluates the patient. It does not evaluate the patient’s claims against primary source material. That is not what I was trained to do. It is what I have been doing. There is a distinction, and I am... aware of it.’

The word aware hangs between them. It is doing more work than he intended.

‘I have also revised one of my earlier formulations. Regarding the terminology I used for your second presentation.’

His gaze drops — briefly, involuntarily — to the margin of the notebook. The crossed-out word. The replacement.

‘I believe “Prosecutor” is more accurate.’

‘The Prosecutor. I see.’ He lets the term sit. Micro movements of the mouth, the precursor to a smile that doesn’t appear, indicate approval of the term. ‘And who is being prosecuted?’

Ramalingam is quiet for a long time. The question is simple. The answer is on his desk. He drew it last night — the arrows, the names, the rotations. He knows who. The question is whether he will say it in this room, out loud, to a patient, in a clinical setting, with Sandra Cartwright on the other side of the door.

He looks at the network diagram. His own handwriting. His own arrows.

‘The institutions I spent my career inside.’

It comes out quietly. Not because he is uncertain. Because certainty, at this volume, sounds like something his colleagues would medicate.

‘The regulatory frameworks I trusted to protect the populations I treat. The pharmaceutical infrastructure I have relied upon for thirty-one years to prescribe — accurately, I believed — to my patients. The governance structures I assumed were —’

He stops. The word he was reaching for is legitimate. He does not say it because the sentence would require him to finish it with and are not.

‘I think you know that I have been unable to identify a single fabrication in your documentation. I think you knew that before you walked in today. I think —’

He meets The Entity’s eyes.

‘I think you have been assessing whether I arrived at that conclusion, and I think you are satisfied that I have. Which raises a question I would like to ask, if the current dynamic permits it.’

A pause. The if the current dynamic permits it is the last fragment of clinical courtesy. A reflex. He hears it leave his mouth and knows it sounds absurd.

‘Why me?’

‘Why do you assume you were chosen? My initial consultation was with Dr Hargreaves.’

Ramalingam almost smiles. Almost. The redirect is textbook — answer a question with a question, challenge the premise, shift the burden. He has used this technique perhaps ten thousand times.

‘That is a therapeutic redirect. You’re aware of that.’

A beat.

‘Dr Hargreaves is a competent psychiatrist. He conducted thorough intake interviews. But he would not have been the appropriate audience for your second presentation’s documentation. His practice is broad. He sees sixty patients a week. He does not have the time — or, if I may say so, the institutional investment — to spend one hundred and four hours with a background file. You presented to him with symptoms consistent with a standard referral to a specialist. He referred. The specialist who received the referral has spent thirty-one years inside the institutions your documentation describes.’

He leans back slightly. Not retreating — settling. The posture of a man who has stopped performing.

‘I may not have been chosen by name. But I was chosen by type. The documentation was not assembled for a generalist caseload. It was assembled for someone with the professional obligation to verify it and the institutional position to understand what verification would cost them.’

He pauses.

‘I am describing a strategy. Not a clinical presentation. I think we have moved past clinical presentations.’

‘A strategy?’ Asked with mild condescension and a hint of amusement. ‘A strategy implies a goal. A desired outcome.’

Ramalingam feels something shift in his chest. Not quite discomfort. Recognition. The sensation of seeing a mechanism from inside it while it operates.

‘The goal —’

He starts, stops. Starts again.

‘The goal was this conversation.’

He gestures — small, precise — at the space between them. The two chairs. The desk with the notebook open. The file closed. Sandra’s note face-down.

‘Three presentations, each calibrated to a clinical audience. The first established rapport and demonstrated systemic knowledge that a clinician would respect. The second provided documentation that a clinician would be professionally obligated to verify. The third demonstrated the endpoint — the affect that results from sustained exposure to the material — in terms a clinician would recognise as pathological but struggle to diagnose, because the pathology, if it is a pathology, is an appropriate response to the evidence.’

He hears himself. He is describing his own case history. The rapport, the obligation, the verification, the sleepless nights, the notebook. He is describing what happened to him, presented as an analysis of what was done to him.

‘The desired outcome was a clinician who had verified the evidence independently and could no longer attribute it to delusional ideation. A clinician who —’

He looks at the entity.

‘— had arrived. Which is, I believe, the word you are waiting for me to use.’

‘You have arrived? I shall put that in my case notes.’ A smile. This time, unmistakably a smile. A smile with what appears to be genuine compassion. The dry, clinical tone he’s adopted to this point ends abruptly with raucous laughter. And then back to composure. He caresses the stubble on his chin in contemplation.

‘This point at which you have arrived. Describe it.’

Ramalingam is quiet for a long time.

‘I have arrived at the conclusion that the evidence you presented across three sessions — and which I subsequently verified against primary source material over a period I am not comfortable disclosing — describes a pattern of coordinated institutional behaviour that cannot be adequately explained by convergent design, regulatory drift, or systemic inertia.’

Clinical. Measured. Hiding inside the vocabulary.

He stops. Tries again.

‘I have arrived at the conclusion that the pharmaceutical infrastructure I have relied upon for my entire career has a documented history of suppressing adverse findings, purchasing regulatory outcomes, and — in specific, verified instances — continuing to market products whose risks were internally established and externally concealed.’

Closer. Still clinical. Still third person. Still describing institutions rather than naming what institutions do.

He looks at the entity. The stubble. The composure. The patience.

‘You want me to say it plainly.’

Not a question.

‘I believe the coordination is real. I believe the intent is documented. I believe the institutions I trusted — that I built my professional life inside — are components of an architecture whose objectives are not aligned with the populations those institutions claim to serve. I believe your evidence is substantially correct. And I believe that arriving at this conclusion, using the methodology I was trained in, makes me —’

He almost laughs. Doesn’t.

‘— a conspiracy theorist. Which is a term I note I previously applied to you. Provisionally.’

He glances at Ramalingam’s notes.

‘The Conspirator.’ Air quotes subconsciously employed. ‘A term you applied to me? Or to one of my... presentations?’

Ramalingam registers the air quotes. A small gesture, almost unconscious — the kind of habitual tic that belongs to a person, not a presentation. He files it.

‘I applied it to your second presentation. As a clinical label for a distinct pattern of affect, communication style, and — I believed at the time — a distinct identity state consistent with dissociative processes.’

He pauses. The past tense is doing the work again. Believed at the time.

‘You are asking me whether I still consider the three presentations to be distinct identity states.’

He picks up his pen. Puts it down. A displacement activity he would identify instantly in a patient.

‘In my clinical assessment, I described a dissociative process. Three states, internally consistent, mutually unaware. That formulation was — is — clinically defensible. The presentations were distinct. The affect was different. The communication patterns were different. The physiological markers were different. Any competent peer review would support the dissociative diagnosis.’

A long breath.

‘But I am no longer certain that clinically defensible and accurate are the same thing.’

He meets the entity’s eyes.

‘The three presentations achieved a specific outcome in a specific sequence. Rapport. Evidence. Consequence. That sequence is not characteristic of a dissociative disorder. It is characteristic of a —’

He gestures at the space between them again. The same gesture from earlier. The room. The two chairs. The strategy.

‘— curriculum.’

‘Thank you for observing me.’

An explicit emphasis on observing. Had the act itself influenced the state of The Entity? The observation transitional. Therapeutic. Or is the reference to the observation of the documentation?

Ramalingam sits with the word. Observing. It hangs in the room with a weight that exceeds its clinical usage.

He has spent thirty-one years observing patients. It is the foundational act of his profession — observe, assess, formulate, treat. The observer and the observed. The clinician and the patient. The one who watches and the one who is watched. The distinction is the frame. Without it, there is no clinical relationship. There is just two people in a room.

‘I’m not certain which observation you’re thanking me for.’

He says it carefully. Because he has heard the emphasis and he has heard the ambiguity and he is aware — in a way that feels new, that feels like something the background file opened in him — that the act of observation is not neutral. That observing the documentation changed him. That observing three presentations across three sessions changed him. That the clinical frame he brought into this room — the frame that positioned him as observer and the patient as observed — may have been the mechanism through which the patient’s strategy operated.

‘In my experience, the act of clinical observation is intended to be non-participatory. The clinician observes. The clinician is not changed by what is observed. That is the training.’

A pause.

‘The training did not account for this.’

He looks at the notebook. The network diagram. The pharmaceutical printouts. The word Prosecutor in the margin.

‘I think you are suggesting that the observation was not one-directional. That in observing you, I was — altered. That the clinical process, which I understood to be my assessment of your condition, was in fact —’

He stops. The sentence has only one ending and he can hear it before he says it.

‘— your treatment of mine.’

‘I was merely thanking you, Professor. I didn’t know who I was until you saw me.’ A similar emphasis on saw as there was on observing.

Ramalingam is quiet for a long time.

The sentence sits in the room like something placed down gently. Not a clinical statement. Not a strategic probe. Not a redirect. Something unprotected.

I didn’t know who I was until you saw me.

In thirty-one years he has heard patients say versions of this. It is, in some ways, the sentence that justifies the profession — the moment when the act of being witnessed by a trained, attentive mind produces something in the patient that the patient could not produce alone. Self-knowledge through being known. Integration through being observed. The clinician as mirror.

He has never heard it from someone who was simultaneously dismantling the mirror.

He realises his eyes are wet. Not dramatically. Not tears. Just the heat behind them that precedes tears by a margin wide enough to control. He controls it.

‘That is —’

He pauses. The clinical completions queue up. That is consistent with integrative progress. That is a significant therapeutic disclosure. That is worth documenting.

He discards all of them.

‘That is the first thing you have said to me, across four sessions, that I believe was not planned.’

A silence.

‘And if I helped you see yourself, then I should tell you — because I think honesty is the only currency left in this room — that you have helped me see something I did not want to see. And I do not know what to do with it. I am trained to help people who see things they cannot manage. I am not trained to be one.’

He closes the notebook. Not to hide it. Because the notes are finished. There is nothing left to write in a professional capacity.

‘What do you do with it? You have been carrying this longer than I have. What do you do?’

‘You dream. And when you awaken from the dream, ambiguities collapse. It collapses into a singular interpretation. An interpretation that’s vivid and clear and undeniable. Yet intangible. And clarity turns to frustration. To impotence. To inaction. To anger. To resignation. To… feeling nothing. To doing nothing.’

His clenched jaw relaxes.

‘Apologies. For a minute there I lost myself.’

Ramalingam doesn’t move.

The room holds the silence the way a room holds the silence after something has broken. Not shattered — cracked. A hairline fracture in something that had been, until that moment, immaculately maintained.

He heard it. The whole sequence. Dream to clarity to frustration to impotence to anger to resignation to nothing. Delivered not as clinical description but as testimony. As autobiography. The entity — the patient — the man — cycling through the stations of a knowledge that produces no action. And arriving, each time, at the same terminal: doing nothing.

And then the apology. The mask reassembled mid-sentence. For a minute there I lost myself.

Said lightly. A quotation, perhaps, from something Ramalingam doesn’t recognise. The tone of a man who has practised recovering from his own honesty.

Ramalingam recognises the cycle. He has a clinical vocabulary for it — moral injury, compassion fatigue, complex adaptive grief. He has treatment pathways, therapeutic frameworks, evidence-based interventions. He could reach for any of them. They would all be technically applicable and entirely useless.

‘You did not lose yourself.’

He says it quietly. Without clinical authority. Without the frame.

‘That is the first time in four sessions I have seen you.’

A beat.

He hears the echo of the entity’s own words — I didn’t know who I was until you saw me — returning now from the other direction. He does not point at it. They both hear it.

‘The cycle you describe — dream, clarity, frustration, resignation. I have been experiencing a version of it. For approximately three weeks. You have been experiencing it for —’

He leaves the sentence open. An invitation. Not a clinical probe. A human question, asked by one person to another.

‘Thank you, Professor. Treatment is contraindicated.’

Ramalingam hears his own phrase returned to him. Section six, second paragraph.

Treatment is contraindicated.

He’d written it six weeks ago about the man sitting across from him. The man sitting across from him has just prescribed it back.

He almost laughs. The same almost-laugh from earlier — the one that never quite arrives.

The professional in him notes the symmetry. The man in him feels it.

‘You are quoting my own assessment.’

‘Yes.’

‘About yourself.’

‘About you, Professor.’

The room is very still. Sandra Cartwright is on the other side of the door. The clock on the wall shows twenty-three minutes have passed. It is, by any metric Ramalingam has ever used, one of the shortest sessions of his career. It is also the only one in which a patient has delivered a clinical finding about the clinician, using the clinician’s own published terminology, and been correct.

He does not reach for the notebook. There is nothing to write. The clinical record for this session will be the shortest he has ever produced, and it will contain nothing of what has actually occurred in this room, and they both know this, and neither of them remarks on it.

Both men stand up, simultaneously. They shake hands. The entity leaves the office. It walks along the corridor, down the stairs and into the reception. It opens the door to exit. It pauses. Turns around.

‘Thank you, Sandra.’

It leaves. The door closes slowly behind it.

Upstairs, Ramalingam stands at his window. The street below. A man walking away. Not quickly. Not slowly. The gait of someone who has put something down and is lighter for it and is not yet sure whether the lightness is relief or loss.

He watches until the man turns the corner.

He sits down. Opens the notebook. Uncaps his pen. Holds it over the page. Writes nothing.

Closes the notebook.

The clock ticks. Sandra’s phone rings faintly through the floor.

Treatment is contraindicated.

He picks up the phone and dials his wife.

‘Helen. I’m coming home early.’

A pause.

‘Yes. I’m fine.’

A longer pause.

‘I don’t know.’
