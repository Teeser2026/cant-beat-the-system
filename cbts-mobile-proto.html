T7 — BLUE MONDAY, BLACK FRIDAY
Late November 2026
Blue Monday, Black Friday

The Waitrose order was perfect again. Not just correct — optimised. The algorithm had learned that Simon preferred the Duchy Originals oats but would accept the own-brand if the price gap exceeded £1.40, that he bought semi-skimmed in winter and switched to oat milk around April, that Amy's wine consumption predicted itself with startling accuracy from the school calendar.

You're not a customer. You're a dataset with a Clubcard.

He unpacked the bags on the kitchen island while Amy talked about George's parents' evening. The boy was thriving. Every metric confirmed it — attendance, engagement scores, predicted grades, the nebulous "wellbeing indicators" the school app now surfaced alongside homework deadlines. George was, by every available measurement, doing brilliantly.

Simon opened the app and looked at the dashboard. Green across the board. A small notification: George has been selected for the Digital Citizenship programme — consent form attached.

Consent. As if you could say no. As if any parent looked at a green dashboard and elected to introduce friction. The form wasn't asking permission. It was documenting compliance.

Evening

The Black Friday emails had started on the Wednesday. Simon counted seventeen in his inbox by Thursday morning, each one engineered with a precision that would have impressed a Cold War cryptographer. Subject lines tested across millions of eyeballs, send times calibrated to circadian vulnerability, discount percentages calculated not to maximise margin but to trigger the exact neurochemical response that converted browsing into buying.

He bought the drill bits. He didn't need drill bits. He knew he didn't need drill bits. He knew that the 43% discount was calculated from a reference price that had never existed outside an algorithm's imagination. He bought them anyway, because £8.99 for tungsten carbide masonry bits was objectively good value, and because the part of his brain that understood marketing was a completely different department from the part that responded to it.

The knowing-doing gap. The central problem of every sermon ever preached, every self-help book ever written, every cigarette packet warning ever printed. You can see the wire. You step over the wire. You see the next wire. You step over that one too. At no point does seeing the wire make the stepping difficult, because seeing and stepping were never connected. They run on different circuits, and the circuits do not talk to each other.

Should I stay or should I go now?
If I go there will be trouble
And if I stay it will be double

Amy looked up from her phone. "Did you just buy drill bits?"

"They were forty-three percent off."

"You have drill bits."

"These are masonry bits. Different thing entirely."

"You said that about the brad-point set."

She was right. He did say that about the brad-point set. The brad-point set was in the garage, unopened, next to the step drill he'd bought in the January sales, which was next to the auger bits from the previous Black Friday, all of them arranged in a neat archaeology of manufactured desire.

He turned off the phone and went to bed. The drill bits would arrive tomorrow.
