Chapter Four

The Study

The desk is wrong.

Helen noticed before I did. She didn’t say anything — she hasn’t said anything for several days now — but I saw her pause in the doorway last Tuesday evening, holding two glasses of wine, and look at the surface of the desk, and then look at me, and then place one glass on the only clear corner and leave without the conversation we would normally have. The conversation we’ve had every evening for twenty-six years, which begins with wine and ends with bed and fills the space between with whatever the day produced. Last Tuesday the day had produced a printout of the Federal Reserve’s ownership structure annotated in my handwriting with arrows connecting it to the Bank for International Settlements’ governance charter, and Helen looked at that, and at me, and decided the wine could speak for itself.

She is not wrong to be concerned. The desk is wrong. The desk should have the British Journal of Psychiatry. Case notes. Referral letters. The Lancet, which I have been reading since 1993 and which I now struggle to open without hearing the second presentation’s voice: ‘peer-reviewed. Published. Retracted when it became inconvenient. The journal is not the science. The journal is the managing of the science.’ I didn’t check that claim when I heard it. I am checking it now. The retraction record for papers critical of pharmaceutical efficacy is — I want to use the word ‘troubling,’ which is the clinical word, the measured word, the word that keeps the finding inside the professional frame. The retraction record is not troubling. The retraction record is systematic.

I am going to write down what I have found because I cannot say it to anyone and because the alternative is to stop looking and I have passed the point where stopping is available.

The background file required one hundred and four hours. I recorded that in the assessment as a clinical observation — a ratio of preparation to contact unprecedented in my career. What I did not record is that the preparation did not stop when the assessment was written. The assessment was submitted. The file was closed. And I continued.

This is not clinically indicated. There is no patient care justification for what I have been doing these past weeks. Treatment is contraindicated — I wrote that myself, and I stand by it. No further sessions were scheduled when the third session ended. The file is administratively closed. What I am doing in this study, at this desk, with these documents, is not professional. It is personal.

It began with the pharmaceutical thread. The second presentation — I still use my labels, though one of them requires revision — brought documentation of the opioid litigation. Settlement figures. Internal communications disclosed during discovery. Marketing directives that used the word ‘non-addictive’ in documents dated after the clinical trials demonstrated addiction. I noted this in the background file. I verified the settlement figures against court records. They were accurate. I moved on.

I did not move on.

Three evenings later I was reading the Purdue Pharma bankruptcy filing. A week after that, the Vioxx internal risk assessments — the ones Merck’s own scientists flagged before the cardiovascular events began appearing in the data. The ones that were, in the language of the subsequent litigation, ‘not adequately communicated to prescribers.’ I am a prescriber. I have prescribed Vioxx. The documents I was reading described a period during which I was actively writing prescriptions for a medication whose manufacturer possessed internal evidence of cardiovascular risk and chose — the word is ‘chose,’ there is no passive construction available — chose not to share that evidence with the clinicians whose patients would bear the consequences.

This is not new information. I was aware of the Vioxx litigation. Every physician is aware of the Vioxx litigation. We are aware of it the way we are aware of Tuskegee: as history, filed under lessons learned, located safely in the past tense. What the background file did — what the second presentation’s documentation did — was remove the past tense. The same institutional structures that produced the Vioxx decision are currently operational. The same regulatory frameworks. The same revolving door between the companies and the agencies tasked with overseeing them. The personnel have changed. The architecture has not.

I paused on that sentence. I had written it as an observation — structural continuity, the kind of thing one notes in passing. But it is not a passing observation. It is the central finding.

I am a psychiatrist. I have spent thirty-one years identifying patterns of cognition — the recurring structures of thought that persist beneath surface variation, that reproduce themselves across different circumstances, different relationships, different decades of a patient’s life. The patient changes jobs, changes partners, changes cities. The pattern does not change. The pattern is not the content. The pattern is the logic that organises the content. My training — the training I am now struggling to trust — equipped me to see this in individuals. I am seeing it in institutions.

The Vioxx decision was not an error. An error is corrected when identified. The Vioxx decision was a calculation: the cardiovascular risk is known, the revenue exceeds the projected liability, the liability is manageable through litigation reserves, the product continues. When the calculation was exposed, the fine was a fraction of the profit. The fine was not a punishment. The fine was the cost of doing business — a line item, anticipated in the original calculation, budgeted for before the first prescription was written. The killing was, by the arithmetic of the decision, efficient.

This is not a description of one company’s failure. This is a description of a decision architecture. And the decision architecture — the specific cognitive pattern in which harm is known, weighed against return, found to be manageable, and proceeded with — is the same pattern I now find in every thread I have pulled. The opioid marketing that continued after addiction was documented. The regulatory frameworks that permit the revolving door rather than prohibiting it. The treaty structures that grant immunity rather than accountability. The policy pipelines that produce frameworks before democratic consultation. Each instance is a different domain. The calculus is identical: we know what this will cost them. The cost is acceptable. The architecture will be built to make the cost difficult to trace and the tracing difficult to act upon.

This is not a conspiracy. A conspiracy requires concealment. This calculus is published — in settlement filings, in treaty texts, in procurement contracts, in the cost-benefit analyses that accompany every regulatory impact assessment I have now read. It is not hidden. It is institutional. It is the cognitive pattern that the architecture selects for — the way a resistance to antibiotics is selected for in bacterial populations. The individuals who apply the calculus are promoted. The individuals who refuse it are rotated out. The architecture does not need to be directed by malicious individuals. It needs only to reward the calculus. The calculus does the rest.

I identified this pattern in one patient over thirty-one years. I have identified it in one architecture over three weeks. The architecture is older than any living participant.

• • •

The revolving door. I have drawn it. On paper, with a pen, the way I used to draw molecular structures in medical school — carefully, with the precision of someone trained to record what they observe. I took the second presentation’s data — eighty-four officials between one regulatory agency and the firms it regulates — and I drew the movements. Not as a list. As a shape. When you draw it as a shape, you see something the list conceals: the rotation is not random. The same individuals move through the same positions in the same sequence. The pattern repeats across decades. It is not a door. It is a mechanism.

I drew the foundation-to-think-tank-to-policy pipeline. The same exercise. Names documented in annual reports. Funding flows documented in tax filings. Policy papers documented in government white papers. The pipeline is not hidden. It is published. Every connection I have drawn is sourced from documents that are, in the second presentation’s phrase, ‘public, verified, and ignored.’

I drew the BIS governance structure. An institution that exists outside the jurisdiction of every sovereign government on earth. Not by accident — by treaty. The headquarters agreement explicitly grants immunity from search, seizure, and legal process. I verified this against the treaty text. The second presentation was accurate. I drew it on the same sheet as the revolving door and the policy pipeline, and the three drawings produced, when placed together, a network diagram. The network diagram sits on my desk between the wine glass Helen left on Tuesday and the Journal of Psychiatric Practice I haven’t opened in eleven days.

It looks — I am recording this with clinical detachment — it looks like the work of a patient I would refer for assessment.

But before the self-diagnosis — before I filed myself under the heading I would give a patient who brought me a network diagram drawn at midnight — I made myself read it. Not glance at it and recoil. Read it. The way I would read a patient’s account before reaching for a label. The way I have been reading everything these past weeks — with the professional obligation to see what is there rather than what I expect to be there.

The diagram has directionality. The connections are not random. The revolving door feeds personnel into the policy pipeline. The policy pipeline produces frameworks that become international standards. The standards become conditions of market access, aid disbursement, and treaty compliance. The compliance is monitored through the surveillance infrastructure documented under System 3. The surveillance data flows through the settlement architecture documented under System 2. The settlement architecture is coordinated by the BIS, which exists outside every jurisdiction it coordinates. Each arrow points in the same direction: toward the centre.

And at the centre — I am going to write this clearly because I need to see it in my own handwriting — at the centre is a single capability. The capacity to know, in real time, the financial position, health status, location, social connections, consumption patterns, and compliance history of every individual who participates in the system. And to condition that participation — to include or exclude — based on criteria set by the institutions at the centre.

The third presentation — the one I labelled the Indifferent, the one that does not use first person — would describe this as emergence. ‘Capital consolidates. Concentration connects to concentration. The gradient is arithmetic.’ I have spent three weeks with the evidence and I can no longer accept that description. Emergence does not rotate the same individuals through the same positions in the same sequence across four decades. Emergence does not produce policy papers at think tanks twelve months before the conditions those papers address have materialised. Emergence does not draft immunity treaties. Emergence does not fund the renaming of eugenics into sustainable development across three generations of the same foundation’s grant-making. Emergence is a river finding the sea — messy, unpredictable, path-dependent. What I have drawn is a canal. Canals have engineers.

I am not claiming I can name the engineers. I am claiming the shape is engineered. The convergence of ten systems toward a single capability — total administrative knowledge of, and total conditional access to, every participant — is not an accident that happened to produce the most comprehensive system of human management ever constructed. It is a specification. And the specification is documented — in procurement contracts, in framework agreements, in the architectural standards I have verified, in the published aims of the institutions whose genealogy I have traced. The specification does not announce itself as a specification. It announces itself as efficiency, inclusion, convenience, security. But specifications do not require announcement. They require implementation. And the implementation is on schedule.

The network diagram on my desk does not look like the work of a patient I would refer for assessment. It looks like an organisational chart drawn by someone who has read the source documents. The discomfort I feel looking at it is not clinical concern for the author. It is the discomfort of recognising that the author is correct.

• • •

I have been using the word ‘Conspirator’ for the second presentation since Session 2. I noted at the time that I resisted the label. I noted the irony — that I was categorising the patient in exactly the way the patient had spent decades adapting against. I used the word ‘provisionally’ and moved on.

The word is wrong.

It has been wrong since I wrote it, and I knew it was wrong when I wrote it, and I wrote it because my vocabulary — the vocabulary of my profession, the vocabulary the DSM provides, the vocabulary of thirty-one years of clinical training — does not contain the correct word. The closest the diagnostic framework comes is ‘persecutory ideation’ and ‘grandiose delusion,’ and I have excluded both on evidential grounds because the patient’s claims are documented and verified and I cannot in professional conscience categorise documented, verified claims as delusional.

The second presentation is not a conspirator. The second presentation brings evidence. Printed documents. Sourced, dated, cross-referenced. ‘I am not inferring. I am reading.’ That is not conspiracy. That is prosecution. The second presentation assembled a case — against an architecture, against a network, against a documented pattern of coordination that my own subsequent research has confirmed — and presented it in a clinical setting because the clinical setting was the only setting where the evidence would be received by a professional obligated to verify it.

I am crossing out ‘Conspirator’ in my notes. In the margin I am writing: ‘The Prosecutor.’

And I am aware — with the cold clarity that comes from standing outside my own professional framework and looking back at it — that this relabelling implicates me. If the second presentation is a prosecutor, then the thing being prosecuted is real. The coordination is real. The revolving door is real. The pharmaceutical suppression is real. The targeting of backup systems with inside knowledge of redundancy architecture is real. The evidence is real. And I have verified it. And I am now a man who has verified it.

I am, by the operating standards of my own profession, a conspiracy theorist.

The phrase applies to me the way it applied to the patient. It categorises rather than describing. It pathologises rather than engaging. It is, I am beginning to understand, a diagnostic category designed not to identify a condition but to contain a conclusion. The DSM does not have a code for ‘clinician who followed the evidence.’ It has codes for the people who bring the evidence and labels for the state of having received it. The labels function as containment. They ensure the evidence remains inside the clinical file rather than entering the professional discourse. I used one of those labels on the patient. I used it provisionally. I used it knowing it was wrong. And I used it because my training provided it and my training is — I am going to write this down because I need to see it in my own handwriting — my training is part of the architecture the patient described.

I opened a new tab on my laptop — the laptop I have been using for three weeks to verify the second presentation’s documentation on an internet connection provided by a company whose ownership I have now traced to the same allocation function documented under System 1 — and I logged into my Government Gateway account. I have a One Login. I set it up eighteen months ago to file a self-assessment tax return. The process took four minutes. It was, as the first presentation would describe it, modern government doing its job better.

I am now reading the Digital Identity and Attributes Trust Framework. Not the press release. Not the summary. The specification. The technical architecture document that describes what One Login is built to do — not what it is currently used for, but what it is specified to be capable of. A single point of access between the individual and the state through which every government interaction flows. Tax. Benefits. Healthcare records. Passport. Driving licence. Right to work. Right to rent. Pension. Voting registration. A single identity layer connecting the individual to every service the state provides and, through interoperability standards published in the same framework, to every service the private sector provides under government mandate.

A single point of access is, by architectural definition, a single point of denial. I did not think this sentence eighteen months ago. I am thinking it now because I have spent three weeks reading the specifications of the systems the patient described. The word ‘denial’ does not appear in DIATF. The capability of denial is present in every line of it. If every government service flows through a single identity layer, then the revocation of that identity is the revocation of access to every government service. The framework does not need to describe this. The architecture is this. The description is the thing the press release is for.

I have been checking procurement records the way I used to read case histories — routinely, methodically, looking for what is not presented voluntarily. The values are outside the expected range. Palantir Technologies holds contracts across NHS England, the Ministry of Defence, the Cabinet Office, the Home Office, and multiple police forces. I already knew this — the second presentation’s folder documented it. What I did not do then was check the company’s origin. I have checked it now.

Palantir was founded with seed capital from In-Q-Tel, the venture capital arm of the Central Intelligence Agency. Its chief executive describes the product as ‘the operating system for modern government.’ That is his phrase.

It is more accurate than he may intend. An operating system determines what applications can run, what data they can access, what processes are permitted and what processes are denied. An operating system built by a company founded with intelligence agency capital and deployed across health, defence, policing, and central government is not a procurement decision. It is an architectural one. And the architecture it installs is — I can see this now, in the diagram, in the procurement records, in the funding genealogy — the national implementation of the same convergent capability I identified at the centre of my network diagram: total knowledge, conditional access, administered through a single layer.

I have a One Login. I set it up to file a tax return. The specification describes a system that could, without any amendment to its published architecture, condition my access to healthcare on criteria I have not been consulted about, revoke my ability to rent property based on a compliance score I cannot inspect, deny my pension based on an algorithmic assessment I cannot appeal, and — if the digital currency specifications I have now read are implemented as described — programme the money in my account to expire if unspent, to be declined at unapproved vendors, to be restricted by geography, by category, by time.

The specification does not say it will do this. The specification says it can. And the institutions that specified it — traced through procurement records, through funding genealogies, through the revolving door I have drawn — are the same institutions whose documented history includes building capabilities and deploying them in ways the public-facing description did not advertise. Every time. Without exception. In every domain I have examined.

I closed the Government Gateway tab. I did not log out. Logging out is a gesture. The architecture does not forget that I logged in.

• • •

I returned to the network diagram. I had drawn three mechanisms — the revolving door, the policy pipeline, the BIS structure — and noted that they produced a network. What I had not done was trace the lineage. I traced it tonight.

I went looking for the engineers. Not individuals — I am not interested in names. Names retire. Names die. Names are the things that change so that the thing behind them does not have to. I went looking for the institutional and ideological lineage — the organisations whose published records connect to the architecture I have spent three weeks drawing.

The Fabian Society published its intent. I have read the founding documents. Gradualism — not as a compromise, but as a strategy. The explicit, stated, published conviction that the correct social order should be achieved incrementally, through institutional capture, because revolution produces resistance and gradualism does not. The inevitability of gradualness. Their phrase, not mine. Sidney Webb said it in a presidential address in 1923. The organisation that said this is not defunct. It is operational. Its membership over the past century reads as a staffing list for the architecture I have drawn — prime ministers, chancellors, directors of international bodies, architects of the post-war institutional settlement. This is documented in their own publications. They are, I will record with the detachment I am struggling to maintain, proud of it.

The Eugenics Society — now the Galton Institute, renamed in 1989 when the word became unsayable, not when the conviction became untenable — funded research into population management for decades before the Second World War made the terminology politically inoperable. The same intellectual tradition — that human populations are a problem requiring expert management, that some lives are more valuable than others, that the competent have a duty to administer the incompetent — did not end. It was relabelled. ‘Eugenics’ became ‘population studies.’ ‘Population studies’ became ‘family planning.’ ‘Family planning’ became ‘reproductive health.’ ‘Reproductive health’ became ‘sustainable development.’ Each renaming corresponds to a public relations problem, not a change of conviction.

The organisational genealogies are published. The Rockefeller Foundation funded eugenics research at Cold Spring Harbor. The same foundation funded the population studies that replaced it. The same foundation is now among the largest funders of global health infrastructure. The names on the buildings changed. The endowments did not.

I traced the think-tank layer. The Council on Foreign Relations. The Trilateral Commission. Chatham House. The Club of Rome. These are not secret organisations. They publish membership lists. They publish annual reports. They hold conferences at which the policy frameworks that subsequently appear in government white papers are discussed, refined, and agreed — before the democratic process that will ratify them has been consulted. ‘Limits to Growth’ was published in 1972 by the Club of Rome. I have read it. Its thesis — that human activity, unconstrained, will exceed planetary capacity and that management of human activity is therefore a technical necessity — is the intellectual foundation beneath every sustainability framework I have drawn on this desk.

The thesis is not unreasonable. The word that concerns me is ‘management.’ The thesis does not ask who manages. It assumes. And the assumption, traced through the membership lists and the funding flows and the institutional genealogies, is that the people who manage are the people who have always managed — the same families, the same foundations, the same revolving door between private capital and public governance, the same calculus I identified in the pharmaceutical thread: we know what is good for them, the cost of their objection is a variable, the architecture will be built to minimise that variable.

The United Nations is the delivery mechanism. I did not expect to write that sentence. The United Nations — which I have regarded for my entire adult life as the institutional expression of multilateral cooperation, the post-war settlement’s highest achievement, the architecture of collective security and human rights — is the delivery mechanism. Agenda 2030 lists seventeen Sustainable Development Goals. I have read them. I have read them the way a clinician reads a prescription — not for what it promises, but for what it enables.

Goal 16.9: legal identity for all by 2030, including birth registration. Nine words. The background file documented this under System 7. I verified it then. What I did not do then was trace the funding. I have traced it now. The foundations that fund the SDG implementation programmes — the digital identity initiatives, the financial inclusion programmes, the health surveillance infrastructure — are the institutional descendants of the foundations that funded eugenics research. The same organisational genealogy. The same transition through population studies, family planning, reproductive health, and sustainable development. The language at the end of the pipeline is unobjectionable. The language at the beginning of the pipeline is not. And the pipeline is continuous. I have drawn it. It is on my desk.

The climate models concern me — and I record that the nature of my concern is not the one a reader would expect. I am not competent to assess atmospheric physics. That is not what I have been assessing. I have been assessing institutions. And when I apply the same method to the climate infrastructure that I applied to the pharmaceutical infrastructure and the financial infrastructure and the policy infrastructure — when I trace the funding, the personnel, the organisational genealogy — I find the same network.

The IPCC was established in 1988 by the World Meteorological Organization and the United Nations Environment Programme. Its assessment reports are produced by scientists nominated by member governments, reviewed by those governments, and summarised for policymakers by a process that is — I have read the procedures — a negotiation between scientific authors and government representatives over the wording of the summary. The summary is what is cited. The summary is what drives policy.

The summary is the product of a process in which the science is reviewed by the same institutional layer I have drawn on my desk. The funding flows through the same channels. The personnel rotate through the same positions — from national meteorological agencies to IPCC working groups to policy advisory roles to foundation-funded research institutes and back. I drew it the way I drew the revolving door. It is the same shape.

The institutional response to the climate projections was architecturally ready before the projections were complete. The management infrastructure — carbon markets, ESG compliance frameworks, energy transition financing, digital monitoring of individual consumption — was designed, funded, and partially deployed by the same network of foundations, think tanks, and policy bodies whose genealogy I have just traced. The Club of Rome published ‘Limits to Growth’ in 1972. The IPCC was established sixteen years later. The policy infrastructure — emissions trading, carbon accounting, sustainability indices — was designed by the same institutions that designed the financial infrastructure documented under System 2 and the standards infrastructure documented under System 7. The same people. The same funding. The same revolving door.

If the models are correct, the management is conveniently pre-built by the institutions that have always sought to manage. If the models are incorrect, the management is in place regardless.

If the models are partially correct but systematically overstated by a summary process that is negotiated rather than authored — the management expands into the space between the science and the summary, and the space is maintained by the same institutional apparatus that maintains the revolving door, the policy pipeline, and the pharmaceutical calculus. The architecture does not depend on the models being right. The architecture depends on the models being believed. These are not the same dependency. And the institutions managing the belief are the institutions I have drawn on this desk, connected by the same lines, funded by the same endowments, staffed by the same rotating personnel, and descended from the same ideological conviction: that human activity is a problem requiring expert management, and that the experts are them.

I have drawn this on a single sheet. The ideological lineage from Galton through Webb through the Rockefeller population studies through the Club of Rome through Agenda 2030 through the World Economic Forum’s stakeholder capitalism — a single, documented, published line of institutional descent, with overlapping boards, shared funding structures, and a consistent philosophical commitment: that ordinary people are a management problem, and that the correct managers are the people in these rooms. Not a conspiracy of ten men with cigars. A network of institutions with published aims, documented funding, identifiable personnel flows, and an ideological throughline that has never been broken — only renamed, reorganised, and re-funded each time the previous label became politically inconvenient.

The pharmaceutical calculus was not an aberration. It was an application. The revolving door is not dysfunction. It is the mechanism by which the network staffs the regulatory infrastructure. The policy pipeline is not influence. It is governance — conducted outside democratic oversight, implemented through democratic institutions, and funded by endowments whose original purpose was to determine which populations should be encouraged to reproduce and which should not.

I wrote that sentence and I did not flinch. A month ago I would have flinched. The flinch is gone. What has replaced it is something I do not have a clinical word for — something between fury and sorrow that sits in the chest and does not move. I have verified every claim in the preceding paragraphs against published sources. I have not speculated. I have not inferred motive from structure. I have read the motive in their own documents, in their own words, in publications they are proud of. The motive is not hidden. It is the architecture’s foundational text. And my profession — my training, my diagnostic framework, my vocabulary — has no mechanism for receiving this except to pathologise the person who says it.

• • •

The fourth appointment is booked.

A request for a follow-up session via email. Reason given as ‘ongoing assessment.’ I replied within the hour. I did not consult my diary. I did not discuss clinical justification with Hargreaves. I said yes because I want to see him.

I am aware of what this is. Counter-transference is the first thing you learn to identify and the last thing you learn to manage, and after thirty-one years I can diagnose my own anticipation with the same precision I would bring to a patient’s. The anticipation is inappropriate. It compromises clinical neutrality. It reflects an emotional investment in the case that exceeds the therapeutic relationship. I can document every red flag. I can cite the relevant literature. I can hear exactly what my supervisor — dead now, twelve years — would say. And none of it diminishes the anticipation by one degree.

Because I have been sitting alone with this. Three weeks. The network diagram on my desk and the wine glass Helen stopped refilling and the Journal of Psychiatric Practice I can’t open and the clinical framework I can’t use and the evidence I can’t stop verifying. Alone. My colleagues would — I know what my colleagues would do. They would use the same label I used on the patient. They would use it provisionally. They would note the irony. They would refer me to someone.

The only person who understands what I now understand is the patient who made me understand it.

I don’t know what to call that. Not counter-transference — that implies a therapeutic relationship, and the therapeutic relationship ended when I wrote ‘treatment contraindicated.’ Not folie à deux — that requires shared delusion, and the evidence is not delusional. Not collegial consultation — he is not my colleague; he is a man who was referred to my practice and examined across three sessions and whose documentation I have now spent more hours with than any clinical case in my career.

He is the only person I can talk to. And he has booked an appointment. And I am, for the first time in thirty-one years of practice, looking forward to seeing a patient with something that feels — that I am going to record honestly, because the notebook is the only document left that is truly mine — that feels like relief.

Helen came to the doorway ten minutes ago. She didn’t bring wine.

She said: ‘Are you coming to bed?’

I said: ‘Soon.’

She looked at the desk. The network diagram. The pharmaceutical printouts. The BIS treaty text with my annotations.

She said: ‘You look like your patients.’

She is not wrong.
