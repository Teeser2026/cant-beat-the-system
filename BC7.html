The Prime Minister

Good morning.

I said last night that my colleagues and I would speak to you at nine o’clock this morning with a full assessment. I am joined by the Chancellor of the Exchequer and by Paul Chichester, the Director of Operations at the National Cyber Security Centre. I will set out the position. The Chancellor will address the financial and practical picture — including, directly, the questions I know many of you have been asking about wages, mortgages, and other financial obligations. Paul will explain the technical assessment his teams have completed overnight. I will close.

I told you last night that what happened was a setback. Overnight assessment tells us it is more than that.

What happened at half past five yesterday evening was not the same kind of event as Monday. Monday’s attack locked our systems. Yesterday’s attack damaged the records those systems held. I want to be precise about this, because precision matters and because you deserve it. The payment terminals that were restored, the card networks that were functioning, the bank systems that were coming back online — those restorations were real. They were working. What happened yesterday did not undo that work. It made the data underneath it unreliable.

I know that many of you spent the last three days believing this was nearly over. I know many of you made decisions on that basis — spent cash you were saving, made plans for Monday, told your children they’d be back at school. I am sorry that the timetable I gave you is no longer one I can stand behind.

But I want to be clear about two things before I hand to the Chancellor.

First: the essential infrastructure of this country is unaffected. Power, water, gas, hospitals, emergency services — all operational, all stable, all continuing. The military and civil contingency framework that has been supporting you since Monday remains in place and is not going anywhere.

Second: we are not starting again. The work done this week — the hubs, the retail network, the fuel distribution, the ARC communications framework — all of that holds. What has changed is the financial picture underneath it, and the Chancellor will address that now.

Chancellor.

Speaker change The Chancellor of the Exchequer

Thank you, Prime Minister.

I am going to speak plainly this morning, because I think you need me to, and because the situation requires it.

On Monday evening I told you your records were safe. On Tuesday evening I set out a restoration timetable. On Wednesday I showed you a tracker moving in the right direction. On Thursday I told you we were on track. All of that was true when I said it.

What happened yesterday evening has changed the picture. The second phase of this attack targeted the records themselves — the transaction histories, account balances, and financial data that sit behind your bank account, your mortgage, your pension, your savings. Our current assessment is that a significant proportion of these records, across the UK banking system and across the twenty-two other affected nations, have been corrupted. I want to be careful with my language. I am not telling you your money is gone. I am telling you that the digital records which describe your money — how much you have, what you owe, what you are owed — are currently unreliable, and we cannot yet confirm the extent to which they can be fully restored.

I know what that sentence means to you. I know what it means to wake up on a Saturday morning and hear the Chancellor of the Exchequer say that.

So let me tell you what we are doing about it, and I will start with the thing I think most of you are worried about right now.

Your obligations. Your mortgage. Your rent. Your gas bill. Your credit card. Your car finance. The direct debit that was supposed to go out on Monday and didn’t. The wages that didn’t arrive yesterday.

Here is the position. No payment can currently be processed through the UK banking system. That means no mortgage payment has been collected from you this week. No rent payment has left your account. No direct debit has been processed. No standing order has been executed. None of these are late. None of these are missed. They are, like everything else, frozen.

This morning I am issuing an emergency direction under the Civil Contingencies Act to every mortgage lender, landlord, utility provider, insurer, and credit provider operating in the United Kingdom. The direction is simple. No enforcement action. No penalty. No interest. No default. No arrears recording. No disconnection. No eviction proceedings. For the duration of this emergency, all financial obligations between individuals and institutions are suspended. This is not optional guidance. It is a legal direction, effective immediately, and it will remain in force until I revoke it.

I want to say that again, because I know some of you need to hear it twice. If you have a mortgage, you do not owe a payment this month. If you rent, your landlord cannot pursue you. If your energy bill was due, it is not due. If your car finance payment has not gone out, that is not a default. No record of non-payment will be made against any person for any obligation that could not be met during this period. You have not missed anything. The system that would process these payments does not currently exist.

On wages. I know that yesterday was payday for many of you, and that your wages did not arrive. I know that weekly-paid workers have now missed a full pay cycle. Employers cannot currently run payroll because the banking infrastructure that processes BACS payments is offline. HMRC’s PAYE systems are offline. The employer is not at fault. You are not at fault. The emergency payment infrastructure I will set out tomorrow is designed to put money directly into your hands — on your devices — regardless of whether your employer can currently process payroll. In the meantime, emergency cash provision is available at every Community Recovery Hub. No one in this country should go without income because the banking system is offline.

On pensions and benefits. The position I set out on Tuesday evening has not changed. State pension and benefit payments are commitments of the Government and will be honoured in full. For those whose payments were due this week and have not arrived, emergency provision is available at every Community Recovery Hub.

Supermarkets and designated essential retail remain closed today while the NCSC assessment continues. Some smaller local stores in some areas may be open on a cash-only basis.

On the practical measures that come next. We are today accelerating the deployment of emergency payment infrastructure that does not depend on the legacy banking system. I expect to be able to set out the detail of this tomorrow, with payments beginning within forty-eight hours. This infrastructure will put money in your hands — on your devices — so that you can buy food, fuel, and essentials regardless of what happens to the banking system. It is being built on the ARC framework, which has been the one piece of infrastructure that has worked, without interruption, since Monday morning.

I know some of you are already asking what this means for your mortgage, your debts, your financial commitments in the longer term. It is the right question. It is not one I can answer this morning. When the full extent of the damage to financial records has been assessed, we will set out the position clearly. Until then, the direction I have issued today means that no obligation will be enforced and no penalty applied. That is enough for now.

Prime Minister.

This is no longer a matter of days.
Speaker change NCSC Director of Operations

Thank you, Prime Minister. I will be brief and technical, because I think that is what is needed this morning.

Overnight, my teams have completed a preliminary assessment of yesterday’s event. I want to set out what we now understand.

Monday’s attack and yesterday’s event were two phases of a single operation. The first phase compromised the cryptographic layer — the authentication system that verifies identity when a card is used or a banking application is accessed. That phase was designed to be disruptive but recoverable. We recovered from it. You saw the recovery on your screens this week.

The second phase, which activated yesterday evening, targeted a different layer. It targeted the data itself — the records, the balances, the transaction histories. This phase was not designed to lock that data or to hold it to ransom. It was designed to overwrite it. The technical term is corruption rather than encryption. Encrypted data can be decrypted with the right key. Corrupted data has been replaced with meaningless information. The original data, in the affected systems, is not behind a locked door. It has been written over.

I want to be precise about what this means for restoration. The recovery work we completed this week — bringing card networks, banking applications, and payment terminals back online — was sound work and it was successful. But it was recovering the pipes. What flowed through those pipes — the data — has now been damaged at source. Reconnecting a pipe to a contaminated reservoir does not give you clean water. That is, in simplified terms, the position we are in.

Our assessment is that the two phases were designed as a sequence. The first phase created the conditions — took systems offline, triggered the recovery process. The second phase exploited the recovery itself — the process of reconnecting systems to central databases provided the activation pathway for the corruption payload. This is sophisticated. It is unprecedented in scale. And it confirms that we are dealing with a level of planning and capability that goes beyond anything we have previously attributed to any known threat actor.

I want to be clear about what is not affected. The ARC authentication framework, which operates at the device level using post-quantum cryptographic standards, has not been compromised. It was not targeted by either phase of the attack. The emergency communications infrastructure — ARC Emergency, ARC Local — remains fully operational. Power, water, gas, and transport control systems operate on separate networks and are unaffected.

My recommendation to the Prime Minister and Cabinet, which has been accepted, is that all legacy banking applications and cloud-hosted financial services remain offline until the full scope of the data corruption has been mapped. I understand the frustration of that recommendation. But reconnecting systems to data we cannot verify would cause more harm than the delay.

We will provide a further technical assessment on Monday morning.

Prime Minister.

Speaker change The Prime Minister

Thank you, Paul. Thank you, Chancellor.

Let me close.

Five days ago this country woke up to something none of us had experienced before. For three of those days, the picture improved. Services returned. Terminals worked. I told you we were on track and I believed it.

Yesterday evening the picture changed again, and I know that the ground feels less steady this morning than it has all week. I know some of you are frightened. I know some of you are angry. I understand both.

Here is what I want you to hold on to. The infrastructure that has kept this country running since Monday — the hubs, the fuel network, the food distribution, the emergency services, the ARC communications system — none of that has been touched by yesterday’s event. It is all still there. It is all still working. And the emergency measures the Chancellor has just set out — the obligations freeze, the emergency payment infrastructure — are designed to carry us through whatever comes next, for as long as is needed.

This is no longer a matter of days. I will not give you a timeline I cannot stand behind. What I will give you is this: every resource of this Government, every capability of our security and technical services, and the coordinated effort of twenty-three nations is working on this. The Chancellor’s emergency payment infrastructure will be operational within forty-eight hours. When it is, money will be in your hands and food will be on your tables. That is my commitment, and I will keep it.

In the meantime, the same three things I have asked of you since Monday. Stay local. Stay safe. Look after each other. Your Community Recovery Hubs remain open — please use them, particularly if you are elderly, alone, or struggling. ARC Emergency remains your most reliable source of information.

We will address you again this evening at six with a further update.

Be well.
