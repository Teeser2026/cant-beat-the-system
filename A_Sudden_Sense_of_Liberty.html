01 / 06

(Sorry for the) Confusion

“You just can’t believe me, when I show you what you cannot see.”

On Tuesday 21 July 2026, Cabinet Office Permanent Secretary Cat Little addressed civil servants. The digital identity policy, she explained, was not dead. It lived on within the Department for Culture, Media and Sport. She apologised for the confusion.

The confusion lasted about thirty-six hours.

On Saturday, the incoming Prime Minister’s spokesman told the press that “all the time and resource that was going to be spent on a national ID scheme will go instead to where it’s most needed, such as helping with the cost of living.”

On Monday, Andy Burnham took office and the cancellation became a headline. Many prominent civil liberties organisations, speaking for three million petition signers, declared the fight won.

On Tuesday morning, Cat Little clarified. The Government Digital Service — the team that builds and operates Gov.uk Wallet, One Login, and the digital transformation programme — would move to DCMS. The digital identity infrastructure would move with it. The teams would not be sacked. They would be relocated.

The same day, the AI taskforce moved in the opposite direction — into the Cabinet Office, directly under the Prime Minister, with a new Director General and a Cabinet-level AI Minister.

The surveillance infrastructure slunk away to a dark corner. The AI that would run on it was paraded out in the open.

Cat Little is not a politician. She is the most senior operational civil servant in government — the person who moves the machinery. She did not contradict the Prime Minister. She clarified. There may have been some confusion. The policy lives on. Different department. Same infrastructure.

Cancelled, apparently

Days earlier, the government confirmed it will ban the sale of high-caffeine energy drinks to under-16s in England from April 2027. Buried in the outcome document were enforcement powers under the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 — RIPA, the surveillance statute — extended to local authorities for covert test-purchasing operations. And a paragraph noting the work of the Office for Digital Identities and Attributes and the digital identity provisions of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, observing that as these technologies develop they “may support age-restricted sales in a range of settings.” The cancelled digital identity scheme is cited as future enforcement infrastructure in legislation published the same week it was cancelled.

A verification apparatus is not built in one law.
It is built in paragraphs like this one, in laws about fizzy drinks,
where nobody is looking.

02 / 06

Week One

“I left the North, I travelled South.”

Andy Burnham became Prime Minister on Monday 20 July 2026 — the seventh in a decade. His first week produced the following headlines, each reported as a separate story.

The digital identity scheme was cancelled. The £1.8 billion allocated to the programme would be redirected to cost-of-living measures.

North Sea oil and gas drilling was approved. The Jackdaw gas field and Rosebank oil field would proceed. The previous Energy Secretary, Ed Miliband, had resisted approval for over a year.

Palantir, the American surveillance technology company, would be dropped from NHS contracts.

A Cabinet-level ministerial position was created — for artificial intelligence — the first time AI had been given Cabinet rank.

A £2 cap on bus fares was announced, funded by ‘diverting’ £454 million from overseas climate commitments.

VAT on domestic electricity would be cut as a “first cost-of-living move.”

Former Defence Secretary John Healey was appointed Chancellor of the Exchequer.

Each headline was defensible. Each was individually reported. None appeared in the same article.

Is it really so strange?

The Maths

The £454 million in climate funding was not diverted. £400 million was restructured from grants to loans — the spending continues, the instrument changed. The VAT cut on domestic electricity saves approximately £45 per household — against an energy price cap increase of £221 in the same quarter. The digital identity programme was already unfunded. The savings announced do not exist in the form they are announced.

03 / 06

The Sequence

“Notice whom for wheels are turning.”

The Green by Numbers series has documented nature, credit markets, and the people caught between them. Each piece tells the same three-step story.

A genuine concern — habitat loss, plastic waste, river pollution, carbon emissions.

An engineered response — a metric, a certificate, a credit, a levy, a register, a mandate.

And the infrastructure that results — centralised data, algorithmic classification, tradeable compliance, digital rails — which serves a purpose none of those consulted were asked about.

This week, the same sequence completed in forty-eight hours.

Announce. The Prime Minister’s spokesman confirmed the digital identity scheme was cancelled. North Sea drilling was approved. Palantir would be dropped. An AI minister was created. Bus fares would be capped. VAT on electricity would be cut. Seven headlines. Five days. Seven separate stories.

Celebrate. The petition signers declared victory. The civil liberties organisations said the fight was won. Everyone responded to their headline. Nobody read anyone else’s.

Clarify. On Tuesday morning, the Cabinet Office Permanent Secretary told staff that the digital identity policy was not dead. The Government Digital Service moved to DCMS. The AI taskforce moved into the Prime Minister’s office. The energy drinks ban cited the digital identity framework as future enforcement infrastructure.

Announce. Celebrate. Clarify.

The announcement is for the public.
The celebration is for the opposition.
The clarification is for the infrastructure.

04 / 06

The Acceleration

“From the past until completion, they’ll turn away no more.”

The architecture documented in this series did not pause for a change of Prime Minister. It did not rebrand. It accelerated. Every cancellation removed a specific friction point that was slowing deployment.

The digital ID brand was generating opposition. Three million petition signatures. Parliamentary questions. Media oxygen consumed fighting the name. Cancel the name and the opposition declares victory, goes home, stops watching. The rails ship faster without the drag of a petition and a brand that journalists can photograph. Cancelling the visible version accelerates the invisible version by removing the political friction. The Gov.uk Wallet continues. One Login continues. The 2027 mandate for all government departments to issue digital verified credentials continues.

Miliband blocked drilling. Burnham approved it. The gas feedstock for hydrogen production was bottlenecked. Now it flows. The CCS infrastructure — pipeline corridors, consumer levies, depleted-field repurposing — was committed under Starmer and continues without comment. What the drilling unlocks is the energy supply for the data centres and the feedstock for the hydrogen economy that the CCS infrastructure enables. The full architecture is documented in the companion piece.

Palantir was a lightning rod. American surveillance company. Privacy campaigns. NHS data concerns. Drop the American name — within days, a former GCHQ director told the Telegraph that Britain needs to build its own equivalent. Same function. Same data. Different flag. Domestication removes the friction. “Britain’s own Palantir” will face less opposition than the American original. Not slower. Faster.

GDS in the Cabinet Office attracted scrutiny. Move it to DCMS — quieter department, less political heat, same infrastructure, less oversight. The permanent secretary apologises for the confusion. Not a demotion. A relocation to somewhere less visible. Faster.

AI had no seat at the table. Now it has Cabinet rank, directly under the Prime Minister. Not continuation. Elevation.

Devolution to Strategic Authorities with planning powers. Data centres, pipeline corridors, energy infrastructure approved at regional level without national scrutiny. The English Devolution Act creates the planning tier. Fewer bottlenecks. Faster.

Defence secretary as Chancellor. Defence procurement meets treasury spending. National security classification available. Faster.

Every cancellation removed a brake. Every appointment cleared a path. The visible version was retired. The invisible version accelerated.

Every headline that says ‘stopped’ means ‘cleared the path.’

05 / 06

The Infrastructure Is Permanent

“So this is permanent.”

The following did not change this week.

The Gov.uk Wallet. One Login. The DVS Trust Framework. The OfDIA-certified identity providers. The digital driving licence. The 2027 mandate for all government departments to issue digital verified credentials.

The smart meter rollout. The Environmental Act compliance architecture. The BNG registers. The Land Registry covenants. The seven-year habitat audit trails.

The FCA’s Digital Securities Sandbox. The tokenisation rails for bonds, equities, carbon credits, biodiversity credits, nutrient credits.

The £22 billion CCS programme. The pipeline corridors with compulsory acquisition powers. The consumer energy levies. The Morecambe field repurposing. The Peak Cluster pipeline from Derbyshire to the Wirral. The St Fergus terminal where Shell’s gas comes in and Shell’s CO₂ goes out.

The water abstraction licences. The data centres. The 850 acres in a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. The 11 million gallons of fresh water per day. The energy infrastructure that feeds them. The AI that runs on them. The data that feeds the AI.

None of this was announced. None of it was cancelled. None of it was celebrated. None of it was reported.

None of it needed a Prime Minister.

The man who implemented Tony Blair’s identity cards in 2006 cancelled Keir Starmer’s identity cards in 2026 and created a Cabinet-level AI ministry in between. The cards change. The direction doesn’t.

There was confusion. It was engineered by the sequence. Announce, celebrate, clarify.

Cat Little apologised. Not for the policy continuing. For the confusion.

The confusion is temporary.
The infrastructure is permanent.

06 / 06

The Machinery

Immediately after this article was first published on Wednesday 22 July, the Cabinet Office published a fact sheet confirming the following.

Responsibility for policy on digital identity moves to DCMS. The Government Digital Service moves to DCMS. A new AI Taskforce, supported by a Prime Ministerial adviser on AI, moves into the Office for the Prime Minister and Cabinet. DCMS also takes on online safety and responsibility for “mis- and disinformation.” The department that now holds the digital identity policy also holds the power to define what counts as misinformation.

A new OneGov Delivery Agency will manage pensions, shared services, security vetting, and recruitment across the civil service. One Login. One Wallet. OneGov. The scheme that would have given everyone one government identity was cancelled on Monday. The agency called OneGov was announced on Wednesday.

The fact sheet describes the restructured DCMS as reflecting “the inseparable nature of culture and technology as drivers of our country and the modern economy.” A second line: “This change recognises that technology and innovation are not separate sectors of our economy — they are the foundation of all future industry, culture, and public service delivery.”

Not complementary. Not parallel. Foundation. Culture is downstream of technology. Public service is downstream of technology. Everything rests on it. Seven Prime Ministers in a decade. The infrastructure did not notice any of them.

The echo is unmistakable. Whether it is intentional is, by Ellul’s own argument, irrelevant. In 1954, Jacques Ellul described what he called technique — not technology as tools, but the systematic ordering of all human activity according to efficiency. Technology does not serve political systems. It absorbs them. Whatever the regime — left, right, elected, appointed — it operates within a technological structure that preceded it, exceeds it, and will outlast it. The system is autonomous, not because no human intervenes, but because the only interventions the system permits are ones that promote its growth. The question of who governs is a political question. The question of what the machinery does is not.

“Whatever the regime, it has its structures in hand. The problem is actually a technological one.”

Jacques Ellul — Perspectives On Our Age, 1981

“When the state takes hold of a single thread of this network of techniques, little by little it draws to itself all the matter and the method, whether or not it consciously wills to do so.”

Jacques Ellul — The Technological Society, 1954

It has been noted.
